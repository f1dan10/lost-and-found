# Lost & Found at MIT

## Problem statement

### Domain

MIT is a dense campus where students, faculty, staff, and visitors move a lot between classrooms, labs, libraries, residence halls, dining spaces, athletic facilities, and outdoor areas. As people move through these spaces, they sometimes lose personal belongings or encounter belongings that someone else has lost.

The parties in the lost-and-found process have different goals:

- **People who lose an item** want to find it quickly and with as little searching and uncertainty as possible.
- **People who find an item** generally want to return it to its owner, but also want to avoid spending significant time figuring out what to do with it.
- **Campus departments** such as MIT Police, libraries, athletics, and building or residence-hall staff receive and hold found property. Their goal is to return property while maintaining a manageable process for the items they receive.

MIT already has an official lost-and-found process, but it is distributed across campus. MIT Police maintains a central Lost and Found in Building W91, at the west end of campus on Memorial Drive, while several other departments also have their own collection points. MIT's guidance explicitly tells people who have lost something to check multiple possible locations because an item may have been turned in to a department other than MIT Police. [1]

This creates a setting in which returning an item depends not only on whether somebody finds it, but also on whether the finder and owner can connect with each other.

### Bad situations

#### 1. A person finds something but does not know what to do with it

Imagine a student walking from Stata Center toward Lobby 7 who notices a pair of AirPods on a bench. The student wants to return them, but does not know whether the nearest appropriate action is to leave them where they are, ask people nearby, contact a building desk, take them to MIT Police, or post about them somewhere. MIT's official guidance says found items should be delivered to MIT Police, while also noting that other campus departments collect lost-and-found items. If the student is far from the police station or unsure which department is appropriate, returning the item requires extra effort.

The result is that a finder with good intentions may simply leave the item where it was found or spend time searching for the correct destination. Every additional step creates an opportunity for the item to remain unclaimed, be moved again, or disappear.

#### 2. A person loses something but does not know where to look

Imagine a student realizes at the end of the day that they no longer have their headphones. They remember using them somewhere between their dorm, a classroom, and the student center, but cannot identify exactly where they disappeared. They can retrace their route, ask people nearby, search relevant group chats, contact several lost-and-found locations, or ask a broad MIT mailing list. MIT's online form accepts a report of what was lost but publishes no list of what has been found, so checking whether the item has already been turned in means telephoning each department separately during business hours.

The uncertainty is especially costly when the item has no identifying information. The owner may not know which building or department currently has it, and the person who found it may have no practical way to identify the owner.

#### 3. A finder cannot tell an owner from a stranger

A student finds a distinctive water bottle in a classroom and wants to post about it somewhere. The moment she describes it publicly, she has no way to verify that whoever replies is the actual owner, because a claim of ownership cannot be checked against anything. Her two options both fail. If she posts a clear photo and full description, identification becomes trivial for anyone who reads the post, and the first person to reply wins regardless of whether the bottle is theirs. If she makes a vague post to avoid this, the actual owner may not recognize their own property.

This bad situation is smaller in individual cost than the first two, but it is the reason that simply creating an open posting board would not be enough on its own. Any system that makes reporting easier also makes false claiming easier, and the problem grows with the value of the items involved.

The cost in any case is modest, but it can be substantial relative to the value of the item: time spent searching, uncertainty about where to look, inconvenience for the finder, and the possibility that an item is never found by its owner. Because losing or finding small personal items is a recurring experience on a residential campus, even a low-cost individual incident can create inconvenience and inefficiency.

### Corroboration

There is direct evidence that MIT's current process is distributed. MIT Police says it maintains a Lost and Found at W91, but also lists other departments that collect lost property, including Campus Activities, Sloan Student Services, athletic facilities, and several libraries. It specifically warns that items turned in to other departments may be delayed in reaching MIT Police. [1]

MIT also asks people who have lost items to provide a detailed description and the location where the item was last seen. This indicates that location and identifying characteristics are already important information in the existing recovery process. [1] The same page shows that the official channel runs in one direction only: a community member can submit a report of what they lost, but no list of found items is published, so an owner cannot check what has been turned in without contacting departments individually. [1]

There is also evidence of students using informal channels when the official process is insufficient or inconvenient. For example, a recent MIT subreddit post about lost AirPods near Stata explicitly suggested using dormspam to improve the chances of finding them. [2] This is a single anecdotal evidence, but it does establish that the workaround is real and that students reach for it in preference to the official system.

Some evidence exists against the need. AirTags and Apple's Find My mean that phones, laptops, and AirPods now locate themselves, so the most valuable items are largely covered already. They do not help with jackets, bottles, or notebooks, but the point is fair. MIT also already has a form and a number of collection points, so a skeptic could say the real problem is that people do not use them, not that tools are missing. That is partly right, and it is why this system has to make reporting fast and cheap for the finder rather than add another place to hand items in.

At the same time, these sources do **not** establish how frequently students encounter the problem or how often existing procedures fail. That is an important uncertainty I would like to investigate through interviews or observation. The specific numbers I would want are MIT Police's intake and return counts per term. In particular, I would want to learn how often people actually use the official Lost & Found, dormspam, group chats, or informal in-person searches, and what causes them to abandon those approaches. I would also want to learn which features of the potential new app would make them more likely to use it.

### Workarounds and comparables

People currently have several ways to deal with a lost or found item:

1. **Ask people nearby.** A finder can ask people in the immediate area whether an item belongs to them, or an owner can ask people who were nearby. This only reaches a very small part of the community and requires the right person to happen to be present.

2. **Use a personal or local group chat.** A student can send a message to a suite, dorm floor, lab, club, or friend group. This can work when it is clear which group the item belongs to: for example, if it was found in a restricted area like a dorm floor or lab, where the potential owner is likely to be a member of that group. However, the item may have been lost outside such area, in which case there may be no obvious group to contact that includes the owner.

3. **Use MIT's official Lost & Found system.** MIT Police provides a central Lost & Found and an online form for reporting a lost item. However, MIT also has multiple other locations that collect found items, so the owner may need to search across several possible destinations. The finder is also asked to physically deliver found items to MIT Police or contact them to arrange pickup. [1]

4. **Use dormspam.** Dormspam is a collection of mailing lists for MIT undergraduate dorms. It provides a way to reach a large audience, and students sometimes use it to post about lost and found items. However, it is a broad communication mechanism rather than a system specifically organized around lost property. In addition, a few aspects of the system create unnecessary friction for someone who simply wants to report a single item: the sender must manually add the dorm mailing lists, and they must follow a set of guidelines when composing and sending the email. [3]

5. **Do nothing or leave the item where it is.** This requires the least effort from the finder, but gives the owner little additional information and may cause the item to remain lost.

A few classes of solution exist outside MIT. Commercial platforms such as Chargerback give staff a searchable database of held items, but they are built around custody: an item only enters once someone has handed it in. Trackers such as AirTags work, but only for items tagged in advance. Peer-to-peer campus apps have been built before, usually as posting boards that still assume the finder has taken custody first.

The most important comparable is therefore not a single competing app but the collection of existing channels: MIT's official Lost & Found, decentralized department lost-and-found locations, dormspam, group chats, and informal searches. They each solve part of the problem, but none provides a centralized campus-wide solution where a finder can quickly publish what they found and where an owner can search for recently found items.

### Solution sketch

I propose a campus-only Lost & Found system for the MIT community.

The idea is to create a shared, campus-wide **lost and found app** that connects people who lose items with people who find them.

A finder could create a report containing a description and optional photo of the item, along with where and approximately when it was found. A person who lost an item could similarly create a report describing it and giving the area or set of areas where they believe it may have been lost.

Because a finder's willingness to help is real but small, this report has to cost less than the alternatives it replaces. The intended interaction is one photo and a building picked from a list of MIT locations, and the item stays where it is. Filling in the location automatically from the finder's phone is a possible future improvement.

The system would then make these reports discoverable to other MIT users. Location would be central to the interaction: users could browse recent found items near a particular location, while lost-item reports could describe an area in which the owner believes the item was lost. A map could provide a visual way to discover found items by location.

The important intervention is **not simply giving MIT another place to post messages**. The goal is to reduce the effort required to connect the two sides of the problem. Instead of deciding which mailing list, group chat, department, or physical desk is appropriate, a finder could report the item in one systematic way; instead of searching many channels, an owner could search one campus-wide app.

The initial scope would be MIT rather than the whole world. This keeps the community bounded and gives location meaningful context: campus buildings and common locations can be represented explicitly, and users can reasonably expect other users of the system to be members of the same community. It also makes the project feasible within the scope of a personal project.Restricting access to MIT accounts also addresses the third bad situation, since a false claimant is then a named community member rather than an anonymous account.

The system would need to address remaining practical issues such as duplicate reports and stale posts. These are important questions for later design work, but they do not need to be resolved in this initial setup.

## Why this problem is worth investigating

The opportunity is not that MIT has no lost-and-found process. It does. The opportunity is that the existing process is **fragmented**.

A lost item can exist simultaneously in several information spaces: the physical place where it was lost, the place where a finder left it, an official department's Lost & Found, a dorm mailing list, and private group chats. The owner and finder can each take reasonable actions while still failing to discover one another.

A useful solution would therefore not need to replace MIT's existing lost-and-found infrastructure. It could instead provide a lightweight information layer that makes the community's existing attempts to return property easier to discover and connect.

## Stakeholders

- **Finder.** A community member who comes across an item someone else lost. They decide in a few seconds whether to do anything about it.
- **Owner.** A community member who has lost an item. They carry the cost of the problem: time spent searching, and the item itself if the search fails.
- **Desk staff.** People at library, athletics, residence-hall and building desks who are handed found items and hold them.
- **MIT Police Lost and Found.** The central office in W91 that receives items from finders and from other departments, and takes reports of lost items.
- **False claimant.** Someone who claims an item that is not theirs. They are rare, but a public post about a found item gives them an opening.
